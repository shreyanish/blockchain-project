A Multi-Level Media Authenticity Verification Framework Using AI-Based Deepfake Detection and Blockchain Provenance
Abstract

**Abstract—**Synthetic media generation systems have altered the conditions under which digital evidence, visual identity, and online communication are evaluated. Contemporary deepfake detection systems frequently rely on isolated classifier outputs, while provenance-oriented solutions verify integrity without assessing semantic manipulation. Binary authenticity judgments produced by these approaches provide limited interpretability and insufficient forensic transparency under adversarial conditions. A multi-level media authenticity verification framework integrating blockchain provenance registration, metadata integrity analysis, and AI-assisted deepfake detection is presented. Cryptographic hashes generated through SHA-256 are anchored to a local Ethereum-based blockchain network using smart contracts, enabling immutable provenance verification without storing media content on-chain. Metadata analysis examines structural inconsistencies associated with editing workflows, while a lightweight convolutional neural network estimates manipulation likelihood from uploaded media. Outputs from all verification layers are aggregated through an explainable trust-scoring engine that exposes the contribution of each verification stage rather than producing opaque classifications. Experimental evaluation conducted on authentic, manipulated, and synthetic image samples demonstrates that layered verification improves interpretability and tamper identification under constrained prototype conditions. The resulting system functions simultaneously as a forensic verification interface, a provenance exploration environment, and a research-oriented demonstration of explainable authenticity analysis.

Index Terms—Artificial intelligence, blockchain provenance, deepfake detection, digital forensics

INTRODUCTION

Trust in digital media no longer emerges from visual plausibility alone. Synthetic image generation systems capable of producing photorealistic manipulations now operate at consumer scale, reducing the cost of impersonation, misinformation, and evidence fabrication. Deepfake attacks targeting public figures, financial institutions, and social platforms increasingly exploit the asymmetry between media generation and media verification, where fabricated content can be produced faster than conventional forensic systems can authenticate it. Global standards organizations and policy bodies have consequently shifted attention from isolated detection systems toward provenance-aware verification infrastructures capable of preserving authenticity records across media lifecycles.

Detection-centric approaches dominate current research. Convolutional neural networks, transformer architectures, and frequency-domain classifiers achieve high benchmark accuracy under constrained datasets yet degrade substantially when confronted with unseen manipulations, compression artifacts, or adversarial perturbations. Classification outputs generated by these systems frequently lack interpretability, limiting their forensic reliability in contexts where verification decisions require evidentiary transparency rather than probabilistic labels alone. Explainable AI research has therefore concentrated on exposing the internal reasoning mechanisms of deepfake classifiers, particularly through interpretability frameworks capable of identifying learned manipulation artifacts.

Blockchain-oriented authenticity systems address a different problem domain. Provenance frameworks anchored on distributed ledgers preserve cryptographic integrity and timestamp verification, enabling tamper-evident registration of digital assets. These systems strengthen auditability and ownership validation yet remain incapable of identifying semantic manipulations introduced before registration or after provenance records are stripped from media circulation channels. Industrial provenance initiatives such as C2PA demonstrate the operational value of content credentials, although inconsistent platform adoption and metadata fragility continue to limit practical effectiveness.

Media authenticity consequently requires layered verification rather than singular detection mechanisms. Cryptographic provenance alone cannot determine whether uploaded media contains synthetic facial manipulation, while classifier-based systems cannot establish historical ownership or integrity guarantees. The framework developed in this work combines deterministic provenance verification with probabilistic forensic analysis through a transparent multi-stage architecture. SHA-256 hashing and blockchain registration establish immutable provenance records, metadata analysis identifies structural inconsistencies associated with editing pipelines, and a lightweight deepfake detection model estimates manipulation probability. Instead of collapsing verification outcomes into binary judgments, the framework generates an interpretable trust score exposing the contribution of each verification layer.

The resulting system operates as a prototype authenticity laboratory oriented toward explainable forensic verification rather than production-scale deployment. Design decisions prioritize transparency, modularity, macOS-compatible local execution, and demonstrable provenance visualization. The architecture therefore emphasizes inspectable verification stages over hidden optimization layers, enabling both technical evaluation and educational demonstration within constrained computational environments.

RELATED WORK

Deepfake detection research has largely evolved through supervised deep learning architectures trained on synthetic manipulation datasets. Early approaches concentrated on visual artifact extraction through convolutional neural networks capable of identifying inconsistencies in facial boundaries, lighting distributions, and frequency-domain anomalies. Benchmark-oriented studies report strong classification performance under controlled datasets, particularly when manipulation techniques present recognizable generation artifacts. Generalization remains unresolved, however, because contemporary generative systems increasingly eliminate the statistical irregularities exploited by earlier detectors. Dataset dependency further constrains reliability, particularly when evaluation environments differ from training distributions.

Interpretability research emerged partly in response to these limitations. Mansoor et al. employed network dissection techniques to expose internal feature representations learned by CNN-based deepfake classifiers, demonstrating that interpretability mechanisms improve forensic understanding of model behavior without substantially degrading detection accuracy. Similar explainability-oriented studies using SHAP analysis reveal that spoofing detectors frequently rely on attack-specific artifacts rather than semantically meaningful facial representations, creating vulnerabilities under adversarial manipulation. These findings challenge the forensic reliability of black-box authenticity systems whose outputs cannot be independently interrogated.

Blockchain-assisted provenance verification has emerged as a complementary research direction. Coppolino et al. proposed decentralized authenticity frameworks in which cryptographic attestations preserve content provenance and verification histories across distributed environments. Parallel work examining blockchain-based media integrity systems emphasizes smart contracts, decentralized storage, and immutable timestamp verification as mechanisms for establishing tamper-evident authenticity records. Research prototypes integrating blockchain with watermarking techniques similarly attempt to strengthen content traceability through persistent cryptographic identifiers.

Despite these developments, provenance systems remain structurally limited when operating independently of semantic forensic analysis. A valid cryptographic record only verifies that media corresponds to a previously registered artifact; manipulated content generated before registration can still acquire legitimate provenance credentials. Recent provenance-oriented frameworks therefore advocate hybrid architectures combining cryptographic verification with AI-assisted authenticity analysis. Mohit et al. introduced a perceptual hash registry anchored on blockchain infrastructure for scalable provenance verification of AI-generated images, emphasizing interoperability with learning-based detection systems rather than replacement of forensic classifiers.

Current industrial discourse reflects similar convergence. Provenance standards such as C2PA embed metadata credentials and origin records into media assets, while platform operators simultaneously deploy AI-based moderation systems for synthetic content detection. Large-scale deployment remains inconsistent because metadata can be stripped during redistribution, and probabilistic detectors continue to generate uncertain authenticity judgments under evolving generative models.

Existing research consequently reveals three unresolved requirements: provenance systems require semantic verification capability, AI detectors require interpretability and contextual verification, and authenticity interfaces require transparent explanation rather than opaque classification. The framework proposed in this work addresses these conditions through layered verification combining blockchain provenance registration, metadata integrity analysis, and explainable trust-score generation within a unified forensic interface.
